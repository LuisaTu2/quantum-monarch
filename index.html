Quantum & Butterflies

Simulating the radical-pair mechanism of monarch magnetoreception on three qubits

Qollab × IonQ Global Quantum Hackathon 2026 · Waterloo node Theme: Quantum Unlocked · October 2026
by Luisa P

Abstract

Monarch butterflies may sense Earth's magnetic field through a quantum effect in the protein cryptochrome: a pair of entangled electrons, the radical pair. We build the three-spin model of Aiello et al. (2026) on a quantum circuit, reproduce the paper's exact result, and then ask what turns it into a compass. With the paper's isotropic hyperfine coupling, the direction of the field changes nothing. With an axial coupling, as in real flavins, the triplet yield rises from 0.50 at 0° to 0.69 at 90°. The circuits run on Qollab's built-in simulator and on the IonQ Forte 1 noise model, where noise lowers the values but the compass trend survives.

1. Introduction

She is a symbol of summer, flowers and ephemeralness: meet the monarch butterfly. 🦋

Every year, monarch butterflies fly thousands of kilometers across North and Central America. Starting from the central region of Michocan in early spring, over the course of a few weeks and a couple of generations, they make their way all the way up to northern regions in Canada.
Then at the beginning of fall a "super generation" migrates all the way from Northen America back to the warmer central regions of Mexico.
But how does the butterfly know in which direction she is supposed to fly?
In this project, we explore the quantum compass that is thought to guide monarch butterflies on their incredible migratory journey. Special proteins called cryptochromes that exist on the retina and antennaes of the monarchs, are very sensitive to blue light. Interacting with blue light creates a pair of entangled electrons, called radical pair. It is thoght that the interaction of the radical pair (and nucleus coupling) with the Earth's magnetic field influence how the butterfly orients itself, effectively creating an "inner clock" or "quantum compass.

2. The Radical Pair

Blue light hits a flavin molecule inside the protein cryptochrome. An electron hops along a chain of tryptophans, leaving two unpaired electrons about 1.5 to 2 nm apart. They are born entangled, as a singlet. The flavin's nitrogen nucleus (N5) then makes the pair oscillate between singlet and triplet. If the pair ends as a triplet, the protein can signal. Earth's field changes how often that happens.

3. Problem Statement

The paper's model uses an isotropic hyperfine coupling: the nucleus pulls on the electron equally in every direction. A compass, though, has to respond to the direction of the field, not just its strength. So:

Does the paper's model respond to the direction of Earth's field? And if not, what is the smallest change that makes it a compass?

4. The Hamiltonian

Three spins, three qubits: qubit 0 is electron 1 (tryptophan), qubit 1 is electron 2 (flavin), qubit 2 is the N5 nucleus. Spin operators are half Paulis, \(\hat S = \sigma/2\). The pair starts in the singlet, with the nucleus 50/50 up or down:

$$|S\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\big(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle\big) \tag{1}$$

The paper's Hamiltonian (Aiello et al., eq 1) has a Zeeman term for the field and a hyperfine term between electron 2 and the nucleus, with an isotropic tensor \(\mathbf{A} = a\,\mathbb{1}\):

$$\hat H = \omega\big(\hat S_{z,1} + \hat S_{z,2}\big) + a\big(\hat S_{x,2}\hat I_x + \hat S_{y,2}\hat I_y + \hat S_{z,2}\hat I_z\big) \tag{2}$$

In this project the field is tilted by an angle \(\theta\) away from the flavin axis \(z\) (paper eq SI 2), and the coupling is axial, \(\mathbf{A} = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, a)\):

$$\hat H(\theta) = \omega\Big[\cos\theta\,\big(\hat S_{z,1} + \hat S_{z,2}\big) + \sin\theta\,\big(\hat S_{x,1} + \hat S_{x,2}\big)\Big] + a\,\hat S_{z,2}\hat I_z \tag{3}$$

We follow the singlet over time, averaging over the two nuclear states \(m\):

$$P_S(t) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=\uparrow,\downarrow}\big\langle\psi_m(t)\big|\,\hat P_S\,\big|\psi_m(t)\big\rangle, \qquad |\psi_m(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat H t}\,|S\rangle|m\rangle \tag{4}$$

Each pair lives for a random time with decay rate \(k\). The triplet yield, the share of pairs that can signal, is:

$$\Phi_T = 1 - k\int_0^{\infty} e^{-kt}\,P_S(t)\,dt \tag{5}$$
Parameter Value Meaning
\(a\) 1 hyperfine strength; time is in units of \(1/a\)
\(\omega/a\) 0.1 Larmor frequency of the field (toy value; Earth ≈ 0.03)
\(k\) 0.05 decay rate, pair lifetime \(20/a\)
\(\theta\) 0° to 90° angle between the flavin axis and the field

5. Building the Circuit

Four gates turn \(|00\rangle\) into the singlet: two electron spins pointing opposite ways, with a minus sign between them. 

def create_singlet(qc, e1=0, e2=1):
    qc.x(e1)        # |1>
    qc.h(e1)        # (|0> - |1>)/√2
    qc.cx(e1, e2)   # (|00> - |11>)/√2
    qc.x(e2)        # (|01> - |10>)/√2 = singlet

The Hamiltonian is written as a sum of Pauli strings with SparsePauliOp and turned into gates with PauliEvolutionGate. To read the singlet probability, the singlet gates are undone: the singlet becomes \(|00\rangle\), so \(P_S\) is the chance of measuring both electrons as 0.

6. Results and Analysis

Press Run on Qollab and main() walks through six steps. Steps 1 to 5 are exact statevector calculations; step 6 sends one job to a quantum backend.

6.1 Reproducing the paper

With the isotropic coupling, the singlet turns into triplets and back again: singlet-triplet quantum beats. The weak field slowly shifts the triplets out of step, so each return is a little lower.

Checked against the paper's exact formula (eq 18): t = 36 → 0.306, t = 37.5 → 0.351, t = 40 → 0.268. Simulation and paper agree to three decimals.

6.2 Tilting the field: nothing changes

Same model, but the field now points at an angle \(\theta\). With an isotropic coupling the molecule has no preferred direction, so turning the field is the same as turning everything. The curves for 0°, 30°, 60° and 90° lie exactly on top of each other.

Largest difference between θ = 0° and θ = 90°: about 10⁻¹⁵, which is rounding error. No compass.

6.3 Giving the molecule an axis

In real flavins, the electron couples to the N5 nucleus much more strongly along one axis, perpendicular to the flavin ring. Keeping only that axis gives the molecule a direction to compare with the field. Now the curves split: the further the field tilts away from the axis, the less singlet comes back.

P(singlet) at t = 4π: 0° → 1.000, 30° → 0.906, 60° → 0.739, 90° → 0.664

6.4 The signal at each angle

We assume the flavin axis points along the butterfly's body, so \(\theta\) is the angle between her body and Earth's field line.

Triplet yield rises from 0.495 at 0° to 0.687 at 90°, about 39% stronger. It changes fastest near 40°.

7. Running on IonQ

Qollab allows one job per run, so six experiments are packed side by side into one 24-qubit circuit. Each experiment gets a helper qubit entangled with its nucleus (H, then CNOT), which leaves the nucleus 50/50 up or down. All measurements come after a barrier at the end, 1000 shots, measuring only the electrons.

exact built-in simulator (ideal) IonQ Forte 1 noise model

On the ideal simulator every point lands on the exact line, so the circuits are correct. On Forte's noise model, gate errors pull the values down, more for longer circuits. The compass trend survives: P(singlet) still falls steadily as the angle grows.

8. Sources of Errors and Assumptions

Status What
✅ Solid The model's maths, checked against the paper's exact formula. Real flavin couplings are strongly anisotropic.
⚠️ Assumed One nucleus, no decoherence, no electron–electron coupling. Toy values ω/a = 0.1 and lifetime 20/a. The flavin axis points along the butterfly's body.
❓ Unknown How cryptochromes are oriented in monarch tissue, and whether monarchs use this mechanism at all.
Realistic models with more interactions predict a smaller direction effect (a few percent to about 25%). Three qubits are easy to simulate classically: the exact result is the reference the quantum run is checked against, and no speed-up is claimed. Each extra nucleus doubles the state size, which is where quantum hardware becomes useful.

9. Next Steps

  • Earth-strength field (ω/a ≈ 0.03) and real flavin coupling values
  • Decoherence and recombination, with different singlet and triplet rates
  • Electron–electron coupling, which can reshape the direction effect
  • Averaging over many cryptochrome orientations
  • The real field strength and inclination along the route from Tecumseh to central Mexico
  • Error mitigation on Forte, and a run on real IonQ hardware

10. Summary and Conclusions

  • Direction needs anisotropy. With the paper's isotropic coupling, the field's direction changes nothing.
  • The signal depends on the angle. With an axial coupling, the triplet yield rises from 0.50 at 0° to 0.69 at 90°.
  • It survives hardware noise. On IonQ Forte 1's noise model, values drop but the compass trend holds.
